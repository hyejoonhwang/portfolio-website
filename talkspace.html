Talkspace Provider Reviews

Helping members see what it’s like to work with a provider before they book.

Overview

As a product design intern at Talkspace, I worked on what members see when they choose a provider. I wanted them to get a feel for what a session might be like before they booked. Could reviews help someone pick a therapist without making providers with few reviews look like worse options?

We started with a link to a reviews page, which is now in development. After the handoff, I designed and tested a card that gives providers with few or no reviews space to explain how they work.

Product Design Intern

Problem

40%of members who were matched automatically didn’t make it to a first session, even with the matching method that performed best.

Solution

Let members pick their own provider and read what clients say about them before they book.

01 · The idea

More members made it to a first session when they picked their own provider

Among members who switched to a provider they picked, 73–76% completed a first session. People who go out of their way to switch may already be more likely to show up, so I couldn’t say picking their own provider was what made them more likely to attend. But it made me want to look at what members needed to make that choice.

Provider cards all showed the same kinds of facts. What I couldn’t tell from a card was what a session with that person might be like. A review from someone who’d already worked with them could help.

02 · Three directions

I tried three ways to show reviews on the card

I kept star ratings off the card and showed what clients wrote. If you see 4.4 stars next to five, it’s easy to skip over someone before you’ve read a word about what it’s like to work with them.

I tested three versions with 18 people over three rounds. All 18 said the reviews strongly influenced who they’d choose.

03 · The constraint

Then our PM checked how many providers had enough reviews. Only 8% did.

Sixty-five percent of providers had just one review. A lot of reviews were blank or as short as “Aaron IS GREAT.” If I put a review preview on every card, most cards would look empty. I needed a design that worked whether someone had 15 reviews or none.

04 · The MVP

We started with a link to the full reviews page

Our PM and I put a Client Testimonials link in the same spot on every card. Members could still read what clients wrote, and providers with fewer reviews wouldn’t have a blank space where everyone else had a preview. It meant one more tap for members, but we could give them access to reviews with the data we already had.

I led a design review with PMs, engineers, and data researchers. Together we changed the link label from Read 15 reviews to Client Testimonials so it wouldn’t show a review count and decided what to show members who tap the link for a provider with no reviews.

05 · After the handoff

What should the card show if a provider has no reviews?

The link made sense with the reviews Talkspace had. After the handoff, I went back to the card. If we could eventually show reviews there, what would a member see when they got to a provider with only one review, or none?

I put a green block in the same spot on every card. If there are at least three reviews that relate to what the member came in for, the block summarizes what those clients said. With only one or two, an AI summary would mostly rephrase what those clients already said. So when there aren’t enough reviews, the provider gets that same space to explain how they work.

I used the concern the member shared at intake to decide which reviews to summarize. That way, a member who came in for anxiety could see that a provider had helped other people with anxiety, even if the provider didn’t list it as a specialty. One person in testing put it this way:

“If they were able to help someone else with the same issues, hopefully they could help me.”

06 · Testing the redesign

Sam had no reviews. Four people said they’d book him.

I tested the redesign with nine people in October. I asked each person to pick a therapist from five cards, then tell me what they noticed and why they chose that person.

What worked

4 of 9

would book Sam, who had no reviews

Sam’s description helped people picture a session with him. As one person said, “I can imagine what the sessions would look like.” Another person picked Priya, who had only a few reviews, because her card explained how she worked.

7 of 9

understood where the text in each green block came from

Seven recognized Lisa’s as an AI summary of client reviews, and seven recognized Sam’s as his own words. Three wondered if the summary showed only good reviews.

4 of 9

chose without opening a provider page

For them, the card was enough to make a choice. Three people chose based on reviews from clients with similar experiences. One said, “the reviews related to my experiences and what I would be looking for.”

What to fix

5 of 9

chose Jane, who had seen the most clients and had the most years of experience

I’d given Sam a way to show how he works, but I’d also left “200+ clients” on Jane’s card. I’d thought of it as a small detail. Some people read it as a sign of experience or popularity. A new provider has no way to match that number.

6 of 9

felt more sure when they could see open times on the card

The current card shows those times up front. I’d moved them behind a button so members would have less information to take in while comparing providers, and people missed having them there.

2 of 9

thought Sam’s own words were a client review

One person thought that meant he had just one review. I’d used quotation marks, which made his description look like a testimonial.

This was a small test: nine people, four who work in UX, and no comparison group. Sam was also always last, which one person took to mean he was the least popular. Six people preferred a woman therapist, so I’d vary both the provider and the card order next time.

07 · What I'm changing

I’m bringing back open times and taking the client count off the card

The test gave me a few clear edits. It also showed me where the setup might have influenced people’s choices.

In the design

  1. Show open times again. Six of nine people felt more sure when they could see them on the card.
  2. Remove the client count. People read it as a sign of popularity, which puts newer providers at a disadvantage.
  3. Mix newer providers into the list. If a card is always last, people can read that as a ranking.
  4. Drop the quotation marks around a provider’s own words. Two people read Sam’s description as a client review.

In the next test

  1. Change who appears as the new provider and where they sit in the list. That’ll help me see how much gender and card order affect the choice.
  2. Keep open times visible in both versions. Then I can compare what changes when reviews are on the card versus on a separate page.

Where it stands

The reviews link is in development. I’ve tested the new card once.

We don’t have results from live use yet. Talkspace plans to track how many members make it to a first session and how many complete three sessions in 30 days.

What I learned

I came in wanting reviews right on the card. Once I saw how few providers had enough reviews, it made sense to start with a link. The redesign gave me a way to keep working on the card without holding up the first version.

With more time

I’d test the redesigned card against the current one with Talkspace members. Would more people book with a newer provider? Would they make it to that first session?
